1 · Heat: whole-window U and the code routes

Whole-window U is a parallel-path problem

Informs D2

NFRC 100 rates the whole product by area-weighting three zones of very different conductance. It is the same arithmetic as a stud wall (Insulation · Parallel paths), and the uninsulated path dominates in the same way.

U = (A_cg·U_cg + A_eg·U_eg + A_f·U_f) / A_total

cg center of glass · eg edge of glass, a 2½ in band degraded by the spacer · f frame and sash

the window's U is the area-weighted average of its glass center, glass edge and frame.

The reference unit is 3 ft × 5 ft with a 2¼ in frame face. Its zones are 9.29 sf of center glass, 2.85 sf of edge and 2.86 sf of frame, so the frame is 19.1% of the area whatever it is made of (Figure 1). What varies is what each zone is made of. Table 2 and Figure 2 compare five constructions.

Key point

Low-E argon glass (center-of-glass U 0.24), set in a retained non-broken aluminum frame, reaches U 0.58: 25% better than the existing-class 0.78, and still above the 0.40 mandatory ceiling. The same glass in a polymer frame reaches 0.27. Thermally broken aluminum, the frame a narrow-sightline interior usually wants, lands at 0.37, inside the ceiling and outside the 0.27 prescriptive limit.

Two cautions on reading the numbers. A quoted "U-factor" that turns out to be center-of-glass describes the 9.29 sf that was never the problem; the whole-window NFRC rating at the actual size is the figure that counts. And the polymer-frame result sits right on the 0.27 threshold, so a margin under 0.02 is within the uncertainty of class-typical inputs.

What the glazing is worth in the energy balance

Glazing is often the largest single row in a house of this era's envelope UA, on a small share of its area (Insulation · Envelope UA by component). Replacing it changes annual energy modestly and the design load more.

Per 100 sf of glazing going from U 0.80 to 0.27, the reference case saves about 266 kWh/yr (about $107) and about 1,900 Btu/h of design load. Annual savings scale with degree-days and price and inversely with COP. A saving of this size does not by itself repay a window replacement. The design-load effect does more work: it moves heat pump selection and widens the outdoor-temperature range over which low-temperature radiant supply water stays sufficient (HVAC · Design heating load).

Formula and reference case
ΔQ (kWh/yr) = ΔUA × 24 × HDD / 3,412 / COP ΔUA = ΔU × glazing area design load change (Btu/h) = ΔUA × ΔT

annual heating energy saved is the drop in UA times degree-hours, converted to kWh and divided by the heat pump's COP.

Inputs from the shared reference values: 2,500 HDD, COP 3.5, $0.40/kWh, design ΔT 35 °F. ΔUA = 0.53 × 100 = 53 Btu/h·°F. ΔQ = 53 × 24 × 2,500 / 3,412 / 3.5 = 266 kWh/yr, or $107. Design load change = 53 × 35 = 1,855 Btu/h.

Figure 3 shows the effect (values in Table 5). At a whole-window U of 0.80 the average surface falls below the dew point of 70 °F, 50% RH air at about 34 °F outdoors. At 0.40 and below it stays clear. These are area averages: a non-broken aluminum frame runs far colder than the glass because the frame, not the glass, is the heat path, and it condenses first. NFRC 500 rates condensation resistance (CR) for this reason, and where a manufacturer lists it, CR is the figure to compare between frames. Frames that run wet on a cold morning while the glass stays dry confirm the frame class at no cost, and persistent condensation at the sill is a mold and finish-failure path that the energy arithmetic does not show.

Where no forced-air stream washes across the glass (a radiant system, for example), nothing masks a marginal window; a cold glass surface is felt as radiant asymmetry from several feet away (HVAC · Radiant comfort). Interior humidity control is in the same guide.

The 75 sf exception is the item most likely to be wrong in a pre-2026 source. It applied in every climate zone under earlier cycles. One city's 2025 alteration form limits it to climate zone 12. 2 · Sun: the glass sets a fraction; geometry sets what it is applied to

SHGC is a property of the glass; incident flux is a property of the face

Formula
q = A · SHGC · I_surface I_surface = I_DN · cos θ + I_diffuse+ground cos θ = cos α · cos γ

α solar altitude · γ |sun azimuth − surface azimuth|, vertical surface

the heat admitted is the glass area times its SHGC times the sunlight arriving on that face, and the arriving sunlight depends on how squarely the sun faces it.

At latitude 37.4° N the noon sun is 29.2° high in December, 52.6° at the equinox and 76.0° in June. On a south face γ is zero at noon, so cos θ collapses to cos α: the geometry that maximizes winter gain minimizes summer gain with no device at all. A west face does the opposite. SHGC 0.70 approximates clear double glazing, 0.35 a typical low-E exception value, 0.23 the prescriptive limit. Actual direct-beam flux is lower at low sun angles.

Key point

A south window admits 3.6 times more beam energy at the winter solstice than at the summer solstice from incidence angle alone (0.873 against 0.241). The ratio is 5.0 at 33° N and 2.9 at 42° N. A west window reaches cos θ 0.886 at 5 pm in June, the hottest hour of the day, against low sun that overhangs do not stop.

One SHGC limit does different work on each face

The prescriptive limit of 0.23 is calibrated to the west case. The code applies one number to four orientations, and the effect on each is different. For 100 sf of south glass, SHGC 0.35 instead of 0.23 admits 3,309 Btu/h more at December noon and 1,262 Btu/h more at June noon (about 0.11 ton of cooling). In a heating-dominated mild climate the December term is the one that matters, and on a south face the low-SHGC glass withholds more useful winter gain than it excludes summer gain.

That conclusion depends on the balance of heating and cooling. It inverts in a cooling-dominated climate or where internal gains make December sun unwelcome, and it disappears where the prescriptive path has no SHGC requirement at all (climate zones 1, 3, 5 and 16). On a west face the same number is correct.

An overhang does nothing at the low sun angles early and late in the day, when γ is large. Title 24 credits overhangs in the performance method through the projection factor (overhang projection divided by the distance from the overhang to the window sill).

Visible transmittance and solar gain are rated separately

Spectrally selective coatings buy visible transmittance (VT) at a given SHGC. The ratio VT/SHGC, the light-to-solar-gain ratio (LSG), is the way to compare them; both numbers are on the NFRC label. Daylight quality, glare and room-by-room targets belong to Lighting · Daylighting. The code's daylight minimum is a glass-area rule, in Part 5.

3 · Sound: the glazing governs the facade until the wall takes over

Soundproofing owns the theory used here: STC vs. OITC, mass law, mass-air-mass resonance, coincidence, damping, parallel paths and leaks. This part applies it to glazing and states only what is specific to an opening. For traffic and rail, OITC is the rating that compares glazing: the STC reference spectrum rolls off below 125 Hz and flatters a window by 5–10 points. How much reduction is needed depends on exposure: level falls about 3 dB per doubling of distance from a busy road (line source) and about 6 dB from a single rail or point source, so a house set well back may need little beyond a good seal, and one close to the source needs the full treatment below.

How glazing and wall share the transmitted sound

Sound crosses a wall through its paths in parallel, weighted by area, so a small area of weak glass can carry most of the transmitted energy. Composite loss comes from the area-weighted transmission coefficient (Soundproofing · Composite loss). Table 9 works the cases.

With a wall at STC 39 and 15% glass, the existing glazing (STC 28) carries 69% of the transmitted energy. Raising the glazing to STC 40 moves composite loss by about 4.5 dB and drops the glazing's share to about 12%: the wall then limits, and 5 more STC points of glazing return only 0.4 dB. With the wall at STC 50 the same 5 points return 2.5 dB. The diminishing return belongs to the pair, not to either element.

What a sealed glazing unit can and cannot do

Informs D2

The resonance of a sealed unit depends only on lite mass and cavity width. Low-E coatings, gas fills and warm-edge spacers do not move it. Lamination does not move it either; it damps the coincidence dip. Equal lites put both dips at the same frequency; unequal lites separate them. Table 10 and Figure 7 compare six build-ups.

The acoustic laminate (build-up E) computes to U 0.30 in a polymer frame, 0.29 with a lower-emissivity coating, and 0.40 in thermally broken aluminum. That misses 0.27 by 0.02–0.03 in polymer. These margins are within the ±0.01–0.02 uncertainty of class-typical inputs; only the product's NFRC rating at its actual size settles whether a unit clears 0.27 or 0.40. The consequence is that the acoustically preferred unit needs a code route above 0.27 (Part 1), and a slim thermally broken aluminum frame uses that route on its own before any acoustic requirement is added.

Laminated glass that is required for safety glazing anyway (Part 5) delivers the damping at little extra cost; the two requirements arise from unrelated parts of the code.

An independent inner sash, 50–150 mm inboard of the retained unit, is the double-stud wall of Soundproofing · Double-leaf resonance applied to an opening. Its resonance falls below the source band (Table 12):

Table 12 Resonance of an independent second sash (6 mm) behind the outer unit, by gap
Outer unit50 mm gap100 mm150 mm

f₀ ∝ 1/√d: halving f₀ takes four times the gap, so the return falls off past about 100 mm.

Gap width is the dominant acoustic variable and nearly irrelevant thermally: the resistance of a vertical air space stops rising once convection sets in (about R-0.90 at 50 mm, R-1.02 at 100 mm, R-1.05 at 150 mm). Center-of-glass U drops from 0.49 to about 0.32. The assembly is field-assembled, not a rated fenestration product, so Title 24 gives it default values and it does not serve as a route to the prescriptive limit.

Watch out

The detail is set by this physics. The inner sash must be the tighter of the two seals, or indoor moisture is driven into a cavity whose far wall is below the dew point. The cavity is vented to the outdoors, through small weep or breather openings in the outer frame, so its air tracks the outdoor dew point; an unvented cavity is better thermally by a trivial margin and worse for moisture by a large one. A retained unit whose own seal has failed (common after about 25 years) fogs between its lites, and a second sash makes that permanent and unreachable.

Other consequences, none acoustic: both sashes must open from inside without keys or tools wherever the opening serves as an escape opening, and the net clear opening is the smaller of the two (Part 5); there are two sashes to open and four glass surfaces to clean; the inner frame consumes 50–150 mm of reveal and reads as a second window. The method is fully reversible and can be tested on a single room, and it is the only way to improve an opening without replacing the unit, which avoids the glass-area cost of an insert (Part 4).

4 · Air and water: leaks are decided by seals and by the perimeter

Operator type sets the seal type, which governs leakage and part of the sound

Operators differ in one way that matters here: compression seal against sliding seal. A casement, awning or tilt-turn sash closes by drawing the sash against a continuous gasket with a multi-point lock. A slider or hung sash has to pass the seal, so it uses brush or fin-pile weatherstripping that cannot compress, and it degrades with use. The gasket that stops air also stops sound. Water is controlled at the perimeter joint

Water intrusion at windows is mostly an installation failure. The unit is tested to NAFS water penetration at 15% of its design pressure, at least 140 Pa (2.9 psf); a PG30 unit is water-tested at about 4.5 psf, the pressure of a wind of about 42 mph (p = 0.00256 V²). The joint between unit and wall is tested by the weather.

Three mechanisms drive water through an opening: gravity, capillarity and air-pressure difference. Sealant addresses all three temporarily and none durably. A correctly built opening handles them with geometry:

  • A sloped, back-dammed sill pan that collects anything passing the unit and drains it outward onto the drainage plane. This is the component most often omitted in retrofit work.
  • Shingle-lapped flashing: head over the weather barrier, jambs over the sill pan, everything lapping downhill. Lapping is geometry and does not degrade under UV.
  • A continuous air seal at the interior, which removes the pressure difference that drives water inward (Insulation · Air barrier).

5 · Safety and structure: where code compliance and adequacy come apart

Two unrelated codes govern an opening. Title 24 Part 6 (energy) covers U, SHGC and leakage. The California Residential Code (CRC) covers escape, fall protection, safety glazing, light and ventilation, wind rating and the garage separation. A unit can satisfy one and fail the other, and one failure in replacement work, losing an escape opening to a smaller unit, is invisible to the energy calculation.

The replacement exemption, and what resizing does to it

The CRC exempts replacement windows from the dimensional and sill-height requirements when the replacement is the manufacturer's largest standard size that fits the existing frame or rough opening, has the same operating style or one with equal or greater opening area, and is not part of a change of occupancy. It still requires the opening to be free of obstruction and openable from inside without keys, tools or special knowledge. How the exemption behaves in each case:

  • Insert into the retained frame. Exempt. A 48 × 48 slider's net clear opening falls from 6.49 to 5.48 sf and its width to 19.25 in: legal, a permanent reduction, and nothing in the inspection record shows it.
  • Full-frame into the existing rough opening. Also exempt, with a larger reference opening. The provision contemplates changing operating style where the new style gives equal or greater area, and a casement in the same rough opening recovers most of the loss.
  • Enlarged opening. Not exempt. A resized opening is new construction at that location, and the full requirements of the table above apply on their merits.

Natural light and ventilation (R303.1)

WindowsDoors

Habitable rooms need glazing of at least 8% of floor area for natural light and openable area of at least 4% for ventilation. A whole-dwelling mechanical ventilation system that meets §150.0(o), such as an ERV, can discharge the ventilation half. Nothing mechanical discharges the light half, which is why a retrofit that removes 12–16% of the glass area in every opening (Part 4) erodes the margin.

Wind rating: PG, DP and window class

CRC R612 requires exterior windows and doors to be tested and labeled to NAFS and to resist the site's design wind pressure. Design pressure (DP) reports uniform-load deflection only. A performance grade (PG) is awarded only when the product passes every NAFS requirement at that pressure: operating force, air leakage, water penetration, uniform load deflection and structural load at 1.5 times design pressure, and forced entry. Required pressures for a two-story house in normal exposure are modest, and readily available products exceed them, so the more useful question is class rather than grade. LC and CW class products carry heavier sections and retain their seals longer than R class at the same PG, and seal retention is what a 30-year service life consumes.

Fire exposure

  • Wildfire zones. In a Fire Hazard Severity Zone, CRC Chapter 7A requires tempered or multi-layer glazing, and §150.0(q) grants a U-factor exception. Most suburban parcels are outside these zones; the state Fire Hazard Severity Zone map shows a parcel's status.
  • Property-line setbacks. Exterior walls closer than 5 ft to the property line must be fire-resistance rated, and openings are not allowed within 3 ft and are area-limited between 3 and 5 ft (CRC Table R302.1(1)). Side setbacks in tract housing are often about 5 ft, so side walls commonly fall in or near this range, and new or enlarged openings on them are affected. The table and any local amendments govern a resized side-wall opening (Verify).

6 · Reframing an opening: gravity, lateral, geometry and code all change

Framing owns the load path, header sizing and braced-wall rules. This part states what an opening in an exterior wall adds to those problems, and what the energy and life-safety codes add on top. Widening or adding an opening changes four things at once.

Gravity: exterior walls under clear-span trusses carry the roof

Informs D4

Engineered trusses clear-span to the exterior walls and deliver the roof load there, so the walls that contain the glazing are the walls carrying the roof. Interior walls generally do not (Framing · Which walls are structural). An exterior header therefore carries a higher line load than an interior bearing wall in the same house, and the ground-floor header also carries the second floor.

The bending demand grows with the square of the span, so widening is expensive in depth. In the reference case, widening an opening from 6 ft to 10 ft (design spans 6.5 and 10.5 ft) multiplies the moment by 2.6 and, at the same member width, calls for about 1.6 times the depth. Where a girder truss lands at the opening, its point load can double the required section modulus (Framing · Reference case).

Lateral: opening width removes braced-wall length

Informs D4

Exterior walls of this vintage are braced wall panels until shown otherwise. In the same reference case, widening a 6 ft opening to 10 ft in a 15 ft wall segment cuts full-height sheathing from 9 ft to 5 ft, a 44% loss, and each jack stud pair consumes about 3 in of wall length. Whether that matters depends on the wall's classification and the bracing arithmetic of Framing · Lateral. A gravity solution does not resolve a braced-panel deficit. Wall classification precedes any decision about an opening in it.

Code status of a resized opening

Informs D4

  • Escape. The replacement exemption does not apply to a resized opening, so full R310 applies to a sleeping room (Part 5).
  • Energy. Added fenestration area triggers the area limits of §150.1(c)3A (Part 1): total glazing at most 20% of floor area, west-facing at most 5%, with up to 75 sf of added area exempt. Headroom is 20% less the existing glazing-to-floor ratio: a house at 15% can add 5% of its floor area, 50 sf per 1,000 sf, before the limit binds.
  • Safety glazing. An opening taken to floor level engages R308.4.3 (Part 5).
  • Permit. Structural work in an exterior wall needs a building permit, and removing or shortening a braced wall panel is among the triggers for an engineer's stamp (Framing · The engineer threshold).
  • Exterior finish. The stucco is cut at a resized opening, so an exterior flashing detail can be built in full there, unlike an insert (Part 4). The changed exterior is also what homeowner-association covenants usually regulate.
Verify

Covenants, conditions and restrictions (CC&Rs) commonly control exterior openings, window color and profile, and grid patterns, independent of the building code. A covenant review settles them before an opening is resized or units are ordered.

7 · Exterior doors: what changes from the window case

Everything in Parts 1–6 about glazing and frames applies to a glazed door: the glass specification is a window's. This part covers what is different about a door as an assembly. Door-specific code is here, and the house-specific entries (which doors, which glass) belong in the House Project Guide.

What differs from a window

WindowsDoors

  • Seal length per area is about the same as a window's (0.97 ft/sf for a 3 ft × 6 ft 8 in door, 0.90 for a 4 × 5 ft window, 1.07 for a 3 × 5 ft window). What differs is the bottom edge: the seal there is a sweep dragging over a threshold, not a compressed gasket, and it wears with use.
  • The threshold is a continuous thermal bridge. It runs metal from outside to inside and bears on the slab edge, itself an uninsulated bridge (Insulation · Slab edge). It is the coldest interior surface in the house, and the whole-door U is perimeter-dominated rather than panel-dominated.
  • A leaf is large, heavy and unsupported over its height. It carries a temperature and humidity gradient across its thickness. Warp opens the seal, and on a sun-exposed face a dimensionally stable core (insulated steel, fiberglass or engineered stile-and-rail) keeps its seal better than solid timber.
  • Doors are operated thousands of times and are forced. Gaskets wear, hinges sag, and security loads act on the lock and strike.

Threshold, water and landings

WindowsDoors

Figure 16 shows the section. A door sill sees splash, standing water and foot traffic that a window sill does not. The water-management mechanisms of Part 4 apply, with the sill pan carrying more of the load: a sloped pan under the threshold that drains outward, a threshold seated on sealant, and an overhang or roof above that keeps the base dry. Opening-specific drainage at a flush or low threshold (as on multi-slide and lift-and-slide units) is the usual failure point of large glazed doors.

The floor build-up interacts with two life-safety dimensions. Raising the finished floor lowers the effective sill height of nearby escape windows (favorable against the 44 in maximum) and reduces the drop to an exterior landing (unfavorable against CRC R311.3, which allows no more than 1½ in below the threshold at the required egress door). Both are checked against the final floor assembly, including any radiant topping (Finishes · Radiant floor build-ups).

Heat, sound and security

WindowsDoors

Informs D5

Heat. Opaque doors (less than 25% glazing) have a prescriptive limit of U 0.20 in all climate zones; fire-rated swinging doors between garage and conditioned space are exempt. At 25% glazing or more a door counts as fenestration and takes the window limit, 0.27 or 0.30 depending on zone, which is an easier target. Large glazed doors are thermally easy: a patio-sized unit reaches about 0.26 on ordinary glazing because the frame fraction is small.

Verify

The opaque-door value of 0.20 and the garage-door exemption come from Table 150.1-A as carried from earlier work. Both need checking against the adopted 2025 table.

Sound. A door follows the mass law like anything else, but a hollow or foam-core leaf has a surface mass of roughly 3–6 kg/m² against 15–25 kg/m² for glazing, so the leaf is usually the weakest element on its wall. A solid-core 1⅜ in door with full perimeter seals is roughly STC 30–35. The threshold is the weak element at the base. For glazed patio doors the seal type decides, as in Part 4: a sliding unit has a brush-sealed meeting stile over its full height that a hinged or lift-and-slide unit does not, so a sliding door governs its wall acoustically whatever glass is in it (Figure 17).

Security. NAFS tests forced-entry resistance separately (ASTM F476 for swinging, F842 for sliding). A multi-point lock draws the leaf evenly against the gasket at three or more points, which improves security, air sealing and warp control together. Laminated glass resists entry through the glazing. Hardware, sensors and access control are in Security · Entry hardware.

8 · Permits and documents

Informs D3

Thresholds are stated where they apply (Parts 1, 5, 6 and 7). This part covers the process around them.

  • Which code. The 2025 California codes took effect 1 January 2026 and apply to permits applied for on or after that date (Insulation · Which code, which cycle).
  • Permit. Replacing a window or exterior door is a regulated alteration in most jurisdictions and triggers Title 24 §150.2(b). Replacing glass only, or a sash only, in a retained frame is treated as a repair by several city forms and usually needs no permit. Practice varies; the local building department's reading decides.
  • Compliance documents. The certificate of compliance (CF1R) is prepared at design and submitted with the plans. The installation certificate (CF2R-ALT) is completed by the installer and given to the owner and the inspector at final. Glazing does not generally need HERS field verification.
  • Labels. The temporary NFRC label showing U, SHGC and VT stays on each unit until the inspector has seen it, with the permanent label. Removing it is a routine and avoidable failure.
  • Product must match the document. A supplier substitution, such as a different coating because the specified one was unavailable, can invalidate the CF1R. A label check against the document at delivery catches it.
  • Plan-check consistency. The window schedule, the CF1R fenestration table and the floor plan must agree: each opening listed with its dimensions, operator type, U-factor, SHGC, safety-glazing status, escape-opening status, and any exception area used.
  • Structural scope. Where an opening is enlarged, the structural permit and engineering thresholds of Framing · The engineer threshold apply.

Where it goes wrong

  1. Losing an escape opening to an insert. The replacement exemption makes it legal and nothing records it; only the net clear opening of the new unit in a sleeping room shows it (Part 5).
  2. Putting good glass in a frame that cannot carry it. Low-E glass in a non-broken aluminum frame cannot reach current limits, and a center-of-glass U-factor on a quote describes the zone that was never the problem (Part 1).
  3. Specifying STC for traffic or rail. OITC is the right metric. Triple glazing is not quieter: three thin equal lites add a second resonance and keep the dips coincident (Part 3).
  4. Treating a prescriptive SHGC as orientation-neutral. It does different work on each face (Part 2).
  5. Enclosing a failed unit behind a secondary sash, or venting the cavity indoors. Both produce permanent moisture on a surface that cannot be cleaned (Part 3).
  6. Relying on sealant for water management. The sill pan, lapped flashing and interior air seal carry the load; sealant is the backup (Part 4).
  7. Defeating egress with a fall-protection device or a fixed inner panel. Only F2090 devices with an escape release are compliant, and an inner sash must open from inside without tools (Part 5).
  8. Assuming a resized opening inherits the replacement exemptions. Escape rules and energy area limits both attach to the new opening (Part 6).
  9. Sizing a widened opening's header on interior-wall assumptions. The exterior wall under trusses carries more, head height may bind first, and the lateral question is larger than the gravity one (Part 6).
  10. Removing the NFRC labels before inspection, or accepting a substitution. (Part 8)
  11. Treating a sliding door as the acoustic or air-seal equal of a hinged one. Its brush-sealed stile is a continuous aperture (Part 7).
