1 · Loads: what the system has to deliver

Bottom line. The design heating load is the envelope UA at the design temperature difference plus the ventilation air after heat recovery; in a mild climate it is small per square foot and falls sharply with envelope work.

Water carries the heat; a separate small air system ventilates

Water carries about 3,500 times the heat of the same volume of air per degree, so a radiant system moves the whole load through ½–⅝ in tubing at a few gallons per minute, and the only ductwork is for ventilation air, about 106 cfm in the reference house (Figure 1). The heat pump and the ERV share no air; they meet only in the rooms, which is why humidity in a radiant-cooled house has to be handled on its own (Part 3).

Design heating load is UA times the design temperature difference

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D2

Q_design = UA_envelope × (T_in − T_out,design) + 1.08 × cfm_vent × ΔT × (1 − ε_sensible)

UA_envelope from the Insulation guide, including design infiltration · ε_sensible ERV sensible effectiveness

heat loss at design is the envelope's heat loss per degree, times the degrees, plus whatever the ventilation air carries off after the core recovers part of it.

The envelope UA by component is built in Insulation · Envelope UA by component; for load it uses design infiltration (ACH₅₀ ÷ 15, 123 cfm in the reference house) rather than the annual average. Title 24 requires a load calculation (§150.0(h), ACCA Manual J or equivalent) using the JA2 design conditions for the location; for heating the design temperature may not be lower than the JA2 Winter Median of Extremes, a colder value than the ASHRAE 99% dry bulb. In climate zones 3–4 the 99% dry bulb runs from the low 30s °F inland to about 40 °F beside the bay (34 °F at San Jose, 39 °F at Moffett Field). This guide uses 35 °F, a 35 °F difference, as its reference.

Load per square foot by floor sets the emitter question

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D1

Splitting the components by floor (garage walls, slab edge, doors and most infiltration to the ground floor; ceiling and floor-over-garage to the second; walls and windows evenly) gives about 17 Btu/h·sf on a 900 sf ground floor and 9 on a 1,400 sf second floor with U-0.27 windows (Figure 3). Individual rooms with large windows or two exterior walls run higher than their floor average, and cabinets and fixed furniture cover 20–30% of a floor, so the output per square foot of active floor runs about a third above the gross figure; the room-by-room calculation is where emitter layout is checked.

Window SHGC decides whether sun or conduction sets the cooling peak

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D3

Q_cool ≈ UA × (T_out − T_in) + Σ(A_glass × SHGC × I) + internal + ventilation after recovery

I incident solar flux, Btu/h·sf · SHGC solar heat gain coefficient

summer gain is the small conduction term plus sun through the windows plus people and appliances.

At 84 °F outside and 76 °F inside the walls conduct little, but the ceiling sees attic air near 120 °F under a tile roof (Insulation · Summer gain). With an average 60 Btu/h·sf incident across faces at the peak hour, the reference house needs about 22,200 Btu/h of sensible cooling with its original glass (SHGC 0.65), of which sun is 11,700 and conduction 8,700; with SHGC 0.23 it needs 13,400, and conduction (7,500) leads sun (4,100). A west room of 180 sf with 40 sf of glass at 180 Btu/h·sf late afternoon still needs about 13 Btu/h·sf, at the edge of what a radiant floor can cool (Part 3; glass by face in Windows & Doors · SHGC by face).

2 · Radiant heating: output, water temperature and emitters

Bottom line. Output per square foot is the surface-to-room temperature difference times a combined coefficient; comfort caps the surface temperature, and everything between the tube and the room sets the water temperature needed.

A radiant surface's output is a coefficient times its surface excess

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D1

q = h × (T_surface − T_room)

h combined radiant and convective coefficient, Btu/h·ft²·°F (ISO 11855): floor heating 1.94, ceiling heating 1.06, floor cooling 1.23, ceiling cooling 1.94

warm air rises off a warm floor and pools at a warm ceiling, so floors heat better and ceilings cool better per degree of surface temperature.

Comfort limits set the surface temperature

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D1

ASHRAE 55 places a floor for shod occupants between about 66 and 84 °F and limits radiant asymmetry from a warm ceiling to about 9 °F (5 °C); a cool ceiling tolerates far more. Because a radiant room has no air stream, cold glass is felt directly as asymmetry, so window U and the placement of emitters near large glazing matter more than in a forced-air house (Windows & Doors · Condensation and cold glass). Operative temperature, the average of air and mean radiant temperature, is what occupants feel, so a radiant room is comfortable at a lower air temperature.

every layer between the tube and the room adds degrees the water must supply for the same output.

3 · Cooling and humidity: the dew-point limit

Bottom line. A radiant surface can only be as cold as the room's dew point allows, and it removes no moisture, so the latent load needs a separate path.

Radiant cooling is capped by the room dew point

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D3

A surface below the room dew point condenses, inside a floor or behind a ceiling where the water is not seen. Controls hold the supply water a margin (here 2 °F) above the measured dew point; the surface then runs a few degrees warmer than the water (about 3 °F for a ceiling panel, 5 °F for a slab), and comfort sets a minimum of about 66 °F for floors and 62 °F for ceilings (Figure 6). At 76 °F and 50% RH the dew point is 56 °F; at 60% RH it is 61 °F.

Against the cooling loads in Part 1, a radiant floor covers an average room and falls short in a west room with large glass; a ceiling covers both while the room dew point stays under about 58 °F (Table 7).

With no air coil, latent load needs its own remover

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D3 D5

q_latent = 0.68 × cfm × Δgrains

Δgrains humidity ratio difference, grains per lb of dry air

moisture brought in by air is its flow times how much wetter it is than the room.

A forced-air coil dehumidifies as a side effect of cooling; a radiant system does not. At a 1% dehumidification design dew point of about 61 °F outdoors (Moffett Field, typical of the South Bay) and a 55 °F indoor target, the reference house takes on about 3,000 Btu/h of latent load: occupants 1,000, infiltration 1,000, ventilation 600 after an enthalpy core recovers about half, and cooking and bathing about 400. With a sensible-only (HRV) core the ventilation term doubles and the total is about 3,500. A ducted or stand-alone dehumidifier, an enthalpy core and a room dew-point sensor at each cooled zone are the means available.

Latent load arithmetic, reference case
Δgrains = 80 − 64 = 16 gr/lb (61 °F outdoor, 55 °F indoor dew point) ventilation 0.68 × 106 cfm × 16 × (1 − 0.5) = 569 Btu/h infiltration 0.68 × 92 cfm × 16 = 983 Btu/h occupants 5 × 200 = 1,000 Btu/h; cooking, bathing ≈ 400 Btu/h total ≈ 3,000 Btu/h ≈ 2.8 lb of water per hour

moisture arrives with outdoor air and from people and activities, and a radiant system removes none of it.

Watch out

Radiant cooling run against an air-temperature thermostat alone has no condensation protection. The control has to read dew point in the zone and raise the supply temperature, or stop cooling, as it rises.

4 · Heat pump: capacity, efficiency, buffer and backup

Bottom line. An air-to-water heat pump loses capacity and efficiency as outdoor air cools and as the water it makes gets hotter, so it is sized at design conditions and run at the lowest water temperature the emitters allow.

COP falls with water temperature and with outdoor temperature

Informs D2

COP ≈ η × T_hot / (T_hot − T_cold)

T in kelvin, at the condenser (water + 3 K) and evaporator (air − 6 K) · η ≈ 0.5 for current units

efficiency depends on the temperature lift, so lowering the water temperature or raising the air temperature both raise it.

Capacity is read at the design temperature, not the rating point

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D2

Air-to-water units are rated near 47 °F outdoor air. Below that capacity typically falls 1.5–2% per °F, and between about 25 and 42 °F frost and defrost cycles take another 5–10%. With 1.7% per °F and an 8% defrost allowance, a unit delivers about 73% of its rated capacity at 35 °F, so the reference load of 27,600 Btu/h needs about 37,700 Btu/h of rated capacity (Figure 8).

At a 35 °F design, derating and defrost are the largest reason rated capacity exceeds the load; hot water recovery during a heating call and distribution losses add to it. Oversizing has costs of its own: a unit far larger than the load reaches its minimum modulation at mild temperatures and cycles.

A buffer tank sets the minimum run time

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D2

V_buffer (gal) = q_min × t_run / (60 × 8.33 × ΔT_diff) gpm = q / (500 × ΔT)

q_min minimum compressor output, Btu/h · t_run minimum run time, min · ΔT_diff control differential, °F

the tank must absorb the compressor's smallest output for its minimum run without overshooting the control band; water flow is load divided by temperature drop.

A 3-ton unit modulating down to 30% (10,800 Btu/h) with a 10 minute minimum run and a 10 °F differential needs about 22 gal of buffer or equivalent loop volume. The reference design load at a 10 °F supply–return drop needs about 5.5 gpm. Many small zones on a large heat pump without a buffer short-cycle the compressor.

Backup heat and the electrical service

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D2

Title 24 (2025) requires heat pump supplementary heat to run only below 35 °F outdoors except during defrost or emergency (§150.0(h)7), caps resistance capacity at 2.7 kW per nominal ton (§150.0(h)8), requires defrost delay timers set to at least 90 minutes where adjustable (§150.0(h)6), and requires thermostats to lock it out above 35 °F (§150.0(i)). Whether the heat pump's controls prevent the compressor and the resistance element from running together decides whether the electrical load calculation may treat them as non-coincident (NEC 220.60), which can move a service calculation by tens of amperes (Electrical · Sensitivity of the calculated load).

Verify

The §150.0(h) and (i) provisions are written around ducted heat pumps; how they apply to an air-to-water hydronic system, and which load-calculation method the building department accepts for it, is confirmed at plan check.

An ERV recovers most of the ventilation heat

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D3 D5

At design, 106 cfm with no recovery would cost 4,000 Btu/h; at 70% sensible effectiveness the core returns about 2,800 of it (Figure 10). An enthalpy core also moves moisture, which halves the summer latent load from ventilation (Part 3) and, in a dry winter, keeps indoor humidity from falling as far. Supply goes to bedrooms and living rooms, exhaust comes from baths, laundry and kitchen, so air crosses the house through door undercuts or transfer paths, which are also sound paths.

Duct topology decides crosstalk between rooms

Heating and coolingHot waterVentilation

Informs D5

Any two terminals joined by a short duct path pass sound around the wall between them (Soundproofing · Ducts). Home runs from a manifold put the full length of both ducts between any two rooms; a trunk with tees joins neighbors by a short path that a downstream silencer cannot reach (Figure 11). Lining, attenuators and terminal velocity are treated in the Soundproofing guide.

7 · Distribution: where pipes and ducts can run

Bottom line. A two-story house under trusses has three routing zones, each bounded by a different rule, and all distribution has to fit them.

Routing zones are set by the foundation and the roof framing

Informs D1 D5

In the attic, ducts and pipes pass through open truss webs or below the bottom chords; no member is cut (Framing · No truss member is cut), and ducts there are outside the thermal boundary and need insulation and sealing. In the floor framing between stories, runs follow the joist bays, and holes across joists stay within the notching and boring limits (Framing · Notching and boring). In a slab, tubing is cast in or laid in a topping; nothing runs below it, so ground-floor ducts come down from the floor framing above or through chases and soffits (Figure 12). Over a raised crawlspace the ground floor is framed: tubing goes under the subfloor in plates from below, and ducts and pipes can run in the crawlspace, outside the boundary unless the crawlspace is conditioned.

Pipe and duct insulation, and the hydronic loop

Title 24 requires insulation on space-conditioning and hot water piping (§150.0(j)) and on ducts outside conditioned space (R-6 in CZ 3, R-8 in CZ 4 for altered systems), with duct leakage tested where a space-conditioning duct system is new or extended past 25 ft; whether that test applies to ventilation-only ducts is a plan-check question. A chilled-water pipe also needs a vapor-tight jacket, because a cold pipe in warm air condenses. The plumbing and mechanical codes govern the loop: oxygen-barrier tubing where ferrous parts share the loop, an expansion tank sized to the water volume, backflow protection at the fill, temperature and pressure relief on the hot water tank, and pressure relief on the closed loop and its buffer.

Where it goes wrong

  1. Sizing at the rating point or at a mild design temperature. A unit rated at 47 °F delivers about three quarters of that at 35 °F with defrost (Part 4, Part 1).
  2. High-resistance coverings chosen without a water temperature check. The floor still reaches its output, at a supply temperature that costs COP (Part 2).
  3. Air left in the loop and zones unbalanced. Without air elimination at the high point and balancing at the manifold, distant loops run cold.
  4. Dew-point sensors in the wrong place. A sensor in a hall misses a bathroom or kitchen zone that cools; each cooled zone reads its own dew point (Part 3).
  5. Assuming the ERV dries the house. An HRV core moves no moisture; an enthalpy core reduces the ventilation latent load but removes none of the rest (Part 3).
  6. A zoned system with no buffer or loop volume (Part 4).
  7. The resistance interlock treated as a setting. It is a product capability that the electrical calculation depends on (Part 4).
  8. Mixing valve missing or set high. Storage at 140 °F without a tempering valve at the heater is a scald risk at the fixtures (Part 5).
  9. Ventilation airflow fails verification. Long or crushed flex and added silencers raise static pressure, and the measured airflow on the CF3R falls short of the design rate (Part 6).
  10. ERV core frost and filters. Cores frost in cold snaps without a defrost strategy, and clogged filters cut airflow without a symptom (Part 6).
  11. A trunk-and-tee ERV layout that joins rooms acoustically (Part 6).
  12. A truss web cut for a duct (Part 7).

Glossary

AWHP
Air-to-water heat pump.
Balance point
Outdoor temperature at which heat pump capacity equals the house load.
Buffer tank
Water volume between the heat pump and the zones that lengthens compressor run time.
Enthalpy core
ERV core that transfers moisture as well as heat.
First-hour rating
Hot water a heater can deliver in one hour starting full.
LWT
Leaving water temperature from the heat pump.
Operative temperature
Average of air and mean radiant temperature; what occupants feel.
Radiant asymmetry
Difference in radiant temperature between opposite directions, such as a warm ceiling over a cool floor.
Sensible, latent
Heat that changes temperature, and heat carried by moisture.
Winter Median of Extremes
The JA2 heating design temperature column used by Title 24.
