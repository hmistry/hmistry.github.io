Low-Voltage & Data

How a house's data, video and control cabling is laid out as a star to one distribution point, where that point and the service entrance go, which cable each application needs and how far it reaches, what power over the cable costs in heat and loss, how wireless coverage depends on wired access points, and what the code requires of communications wiring in a dwelling.

1 · Applications: what a house wires for

Bottom line. Every application that leaves the walls finished needs its conductor in place before drywall; what differs between them is the medium, where the run ends and whether it carries power.

Each application has a medium and an end point

Informs D1 D4

Data outlets serve fixed devices that move large amounts of data or need steady latency: televisions, desks, game consoles and storage. Wireless access points take a run to the ceiling, which also powers them (Part 6). Cameras, doorbells and entry keypads take runs to the exterior and the entries (Security · Cameras). Equipment in the energy system increasingly takes a wired connection: a storage gateway, an inverter, an EV charger and heat pump or ERV controllers (Solar & Battery · Backup scope). A relay lighting system takes keypad runs (Lighting · Architectures). Audio takes speaker cable, and an antenna or a cable provider takes coax. Table 2 lists the medium for each.

2 · Topology and the service entrance

Bottom line. A star of home runs to one distribution point, fed from a provider entrance that is bonded near the electrical service, is the arrangement ANSI/TIA-568 and TIA-570 draw; the two locations are chosen for different reasons.

A home run confines a fault to one outlet

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D1 D2

In a star each outlet has its own cable to a patch panel at the distribution point. A failed jack, a nicked cable or a re-termination affects one outlet, and any outlet can be repatched from data to phone, camera or access point without touching another. A daisy chain or a coax splitter tree passes every downstream outlet through each upstream connection, so one fault darkens everything after it (Figure 1). Ethernet requires a star; the choice the house makes is how many points and where.

A metallic entrance bonds within 20 ft or to its own electrode; the distribution point is not tied to it

Informs D2 D3

A provider's metallic cable, telephone pair or coax enters through a listed primary protector or grounding block placed as close as practicable to the point of entrance, and its grounding conductor, at least 14 AWG copper, runs to the intersystem bonding termination at the service (250.94; Electrical · Grounding and bonding). In a one- or two-family dwelling that conductor is limited to 20 ft; beyond it, a separate electrode at least 5 ft long is driven at the entrance and bonded to the power electrode system with 6 AWG (800.100). The bond keeps the communications shield and the power ground at one potential during a surge or a fault. A fiber drop with no metallic member needs no protector or bond.

The protector therefore sits near the electrical service, while the distribution point belongs where the home runs are short and the equipment can shed heat (Part 5). One cable, a coax or a Cat6A, joins the two. Equipment already landing at the service, a storage gateway and the power control wiring at the panel, takes a run from the distribution point back to the service (Figure 2).

Key point

Siting the distribution point at the service, as the bonding rule might suggest, is not required by it; only the protector and its bond are.

3 · Cable, speed and reach

Bottom line. Category sets the speed a run can carry over a given length; in a house the lengths are short enough that the category choice is about headroom for later equipment and PoE heat, not reach.

Category, coax and fiber differ in speed, reach and what they carry

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D4

Twisted-pair Ethernet runs over a channel of a permanent link (up to 90 m, 295 ft) and patch cords (up to 10 m), 100 m (328 ft) in all (ANSI/TIA-568.2-D). Speed at that length depends on category: Cat5e carries 1 Gb/s and, under IEEE 802.3bz, 2.5 Gb/s; Cat6 carries 5 Gb/s at full length and 10 Gb/s to about 55 m (180 ft) where alien crosstalk is low; Cat6A carries 10 Gb/s at full length. Cat6A's larger conductors and jacket also run cooler under PoE (Part 4). Coax carries an antenna or a cable provider's signal and, with MoCA adapters, Ethernet where no twisted pair can be pulled. Fiber carries a provider's service to the optical network terminal and, inside, any speed over any house distance; it is used where an outbuilding or a run beside power makes copper unsuitable. Routed length in a house stays well inside the channel limit

Informs D2 D4

L_channel = k × (M + V) + s + c

M horizontal Manhattan distance, ft · V vertical path, ft · k routing factor for detours through framing, about 1.2–1.3 · s slack, about 3 ft at the distribution point and 1 ft at the outlet · c patch cords, about 10 ft

add the distances along the walls and floors, stretch them for the detours framing forces, then add the slack and the cords.

On the reference house, a 40 × 35 ft upper floor with a 9 ft floor-to-floor height, a central distribution point reaches the farthest outlet on either floor with a channel of about 74 ft. Moved to a corner, it reaches the far corner in about 121 ft, and a camera at the far eave by way of the attic in about 125 ft (Figure 3). Every case sits under Cat6's 180 ft at 10 Gb/s and under half the 328 ft limit. From a corner, the 180 ft reach binds only once the footprint's length plus width exceeds about 119 ft. The routing factor is the sensitive input: at 1.5 instead of 1.25 the longest case grows by about a fifth.

Run lengths for the reference cases

A: 1.25 × (37.5 + 10.5) + 4 + 10 = 74 ft; B: 1.25 × (37.5 + 5.5) + 4 + 10 = 68 ft; C: 1.25 × (75.0 + 5.5) + 4 + 10 = 115 ft; D: 1.25 × (75.0 + 10.5) + 4 + 10 = 121 ft; E: 1.25 × (75.0 + 14.0) + 4 + 10 = 125 ft. Central Manhattan distance 20 + 17.5 = 37.5 ft; corner 40 + 35 = 75 ft. Vertical: 3.5 ft up to the floor cavity from an enclosure top at 5 ft, 7 ft down to a ground-floor outlet, 2 ft up to a second-floor outlet; 14 ft up to the attic and down to an eave. Break-even Manhattan distance for 180 ft: (180 − 10 − 4) ÷ 1.25 − 14 = 119 ft.

4 · Power over the cable

Bottom line. PoE delivers power to a device over its data cable; what bounds it is the switch's total budget and the heat in bundles of powered cables, while loss over a house-length run stays a few watts.

Four PoE types set power at the switch and at the device

Informs D5

IEEE 802.3 defines four types by the power the switch port (the PSE) supplies and the minimum the device receives after a worst-case 100 m channel (Table 5). Types 1 and 2 use two pairs; Types 3 and 4 can use all four. A switch negotiates a class with each device and either reserves the class maximum or allocates by measured draw, depending on the switch.

Cable loss grows with current squared and length

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D4 D5

P_loss = I² × R_loop; R_loop = r × L (two pairs) or r × L ÷ 2 (four pairs)

I current to the device, A · r resistance of one conductor, about 0.020 Ω/ft for 23 AWG at 20 °C (0.026 for 24 AWG) · L run length, ft

each pair carries current on two conductors in parallel, out on one pair and back on another, so loss rises with the square of the current and in proportion to length.

At full current, taking the worst-case pair current, a Type 2 port loses about 0.7 W per 100 ft of Cat6 and a Type 4 port about 3.7 W (Figure 4). Over the reference house's longest run, 125 ft, a Type 4 access point at full draw would lose about 4.7 W, about 5% of the port's 90 W; a Type 2 camera under 1 W. The loss is heat inside the wall and in the bundle. Copper resistance rises about 0.4% per °C, so a warm bundle loses a little more.

The code limits powered bundles only above 0.3 A per conductor

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D5 D7

Cables carrying power in a tight bundle warm each other, and the core of a large bundle runs hottest. 725.144 limits the current per conductor in bundles of Class 2 and Class 3 cables carrying power and data by conductor size, insulation temperature rating and the number of cables bundled, or allows cable listed and marked LP with its current rating. Where the conductors are 24 AWG or larger and the power source's rated current does not exceed 0.3 A per conductor, those limits do not apply. The test is the port's rating, not the device's draw: ports rated for Types 1 to 3 supply at most 0.3 A per conductor, and a port rated for Type 4 up to about 0.43 A balanced (0.48 A on the worst-case pair), whatever it feeds. In bundles of up to 37 cables every common horizontal cable meets the table at Type 4 current; the reference set's 38 cables sit at the step where gauge and temperature rating begin to matter. The table assumes 30 °C ambient, so a bundle through a hot attic is derated.

Verify

The 0.3 A exception and the Table 725.144 values are as stated for the 2020 NEC and carried into the 2023 edition after the reorganization into Articles 722, 724 and 725; confirm the adopted text and the LP listing route against the 2025 CEC.

The switch budget is the sum of reserved classes or of measured draw

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D2 D5

A switch has a total PoE budget, often 65, 120 or 250 W (typical); Wi-Fi 7 access points commonly need Type 3 power. For the reference device set in Table 6, class reservations total 137 W while typical draw is about 63 W. A switch that reserves by class needs the 250 W tier; one that allocates by measured draw fits 120 W with margin for an access point's peaks. The switch's base load and conversion loss, about a tenth of the power delivered, are its heat at the distribution point; base load plus delivered power is its draw on backup power (Part 5).

5 · Equipment: heat, power and backup

Bottom line. The distribution point holds passive termination and, usually, active equipment; the active part sets the enclosure's heat, its circuit, and its draw on backup power.

An enclosure's temperature rise is its dissipated power over its heat-loss coefficient

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D2

ΔT = P ÷ (h × A)

P dissipated power, W · h combined convection and radiation coefficient, about 5–10 W/m²·K for a still surface; 7 is used here, on the conservative side · A area exposed to room air, m²

the enclosure warms until the heat leaving its surfaces equals the heat its equipment makes.

Most of what active equipment draws becomes heat in place. A switch base load, PoE conversion losses, a router and a modem or ONT make about 43 W; a network video recorder adds about 25 W (typical). A recessed media enclosure 14 × 30 in exposes about 0.3 m² of door to the room and a little more to the stud bay, for an effective h × A near 2.5 W/K, so 43 W lifts it about 31 °F and 68 W about 49 °F. Much network equipment is rated to about 104 °F (40 °C) ambient (typical), which a closed in-wall enclosure in a 75 °F room exceeds near 40 W. A closet whose walls and door exchange heat with the house has about ten times the coefficient and stays within a few degrees (Figure 5). Passive termination, a patch panel and splitters, makes no heat and fits any enclosure.

Enclosure heat inputs

Switch, base load 15 W, PoE conversion, ~10% of delivered 6 W, Router 12 W, Modem or ONT 10 W = 43 W; recorder 25 W; total 68 W. h × A: closed in-wall enclosure 2.5 W/K (0.3 m² of door at about 7 W/m²·K, plus a share through the stud bay); vented door 5 W/K; closet with the door closed 25 W/K, from about 8 m² of gypsum surfaces at about 3 W/m²·K to adjoining rooms. ΔT in °F = P ÷ hA × 1.8.

Always-on equipment is a continuous load on the bill and on backup

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D2

Network equipment, PoE devices and a recorder run continuously. The reference equipment with a recorder, 68 W, and its powered devices, 63 W, total about 131 W: about 1,148 kWh a year, $459 at $0.40/kWh, and 3.1 kWh a day, about 23% of a 13.5 kWh storage unit's energy for each day of an outage (Table 7; Solar & Battery · Autonomy). Whether the distribution point stays up in an outage depends on its circuit being among those the storage backs up; a small UPS bridges the transfer and covers an outage on its own for an hour or two at this load (typical).

Active equipment at the distribution point needs a receptacle, access and a heat path

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D2

Active equipment takes a receptacle inside or beside the enclosure; a circuit shared with nothing likely to trip it, and included in the backed-up loads, keeps the network up through faults elsewhere and outages. The patch panel needs working access for repatching, and slack of about 3 ft per cable inside the enclosure lets a jack be re-terminated. Power and communications share an enclosure only with a barrier or listed divider between them (Part 8). A location away from bedrooms keeps fan noise out of them; small switches are often fanless, larger PoE switches are not.

6 · Wireless: access points and backhaul

Bottom line. Wireless reach falls fastest at the higher bands and through floors, and the client's weaker transmitter sets the useful range; coverage at 5 and 6 GHz therefore follows from access points on each floor, wired back to the distribution point.

Reach by band, on one floor and through one floor

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D6

L = 20 log₁₀ f + N log₁₀ d − 28 + L_f

L path loss, dB · f frequency, MHz · N distance coefficient, about 28–35 in houses, higher at higher bands · d distance, m · L_f floor penetration loss, dB

loss grows with frequency, with distance at a rate set by the walls and furniture, and in a step for each floor.

The link budget is the one used for control radios (Lighting · Radio links); this site-general indoor form folds typical walls into N, with assumed values on the conservative side for wood-frame houses and a 2.4 GHz floor loss of 8 dB as in the Lighting guide. The limiting direction is the client's: a phone or laptop transmits about 15 dBm against an access point's 20 dBm or more, so raising the access point's power extends what it hears no further. For -67 dBm at the access point, a common target for video and calls, reach on the same floor is about 12 m at 5 GHz and 10 m at 6 GHz, and through one floor 5.0 m and 3.8 m (Figure 6). A ceiling access point at the center of the reference house's 40 × 35 ft floor is about 8.2 m from the farthest corner of its own floor, inside 5 GHz reach and near the edge at 6 GHz; serving the other floor from it relies on 2.4 GHz. With N = 28, the lower value in ITU-R P.1238's residential guidance, same-floor 5 GHz reach grows to about 18 m. Values vary by ±10 dB with construction; coated glass and stucco on metal lath affect outdoor links, not indoor ones.

Wireless reach inputs

Client transmit 15 dBm; allowed loss 82 dB. 2.4 GHz: f = 2440 MHz, N = 30, floor 8 dB, reach 25.6 m same floor, 13.9 m through a floor; 5 GHz: f = 5500 MHz, N = 33, floor 12 dB, reach 11.7 m same floor, 5.0 m through a floor; 6 GHz: f = 6100 MHz, N = 35, floor 14 dB, reach 9.5 m same floor, 3.8 m through a floor. Distance coefficients and floor losses assumed, conservative for wood-frame houses; ITU-R P.1238 gives about 28 for residential buildings and floor factors measured on concrete. Farthest point: √(20² + 17.5²) = 26.6 ft = 8.1 m horizontally, 8.2 m with 1.5 m of height difference.

A wireless hop shares airtime that a wired drop does not

Informs D6

A mesh node without a wired drop relays traffic over the air. On a node with one radio for both clients and backhaul, each hop roughly halves the throughput available beyond it (typical); a dedicated backhaul radio avoids the halving but still depends on the path between nodes, which the floor loss above governs. A wired drop to each access point removes the hop and lets the cable power the access point as well. Ceiling mounting puts the antenna above furniture and people, which absorb 2.4–6 GHz signals.

7 · Pathways: sleeves, fill, fire and air

Bottom line. The cable plant is only as extensible as its pathways; a sleeve or conduit at each crossing of a floor or top plate is what allows a later pull, and each crossing of a fire separation or the air barrier is sealed.

A sleeve at every floor and plate crossing keeps the plant extensible

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D7

Cables cross the top plate and the floor between stories in bored holes or sleeves. The bore sizes and locations are limited by the framing rules, and no truss member is drilled (Framing · Notching and boring). A sleeve, a short length of conduit through the plate with a bushing, is left in place with a pull string, so a later cable follows the same path. The reference set's 38 cables, about 1.7 sq in, enter the distribution point through 2 sleeves of 2 in at 40% fill, or an equivalent number of bores (Figure 7). A 1 in conduit from a media location or the attic holds seven Cat6 or five Cat6A at 40% fill (Table 8).

Fill, bend radius and tension limit how cable is pulled

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D7

Industry practice fills a conduit with three or more cables to 40% of its internal area (ANSI/TIA-569). Table 8 gives the counts. A twisted-pair cable bends no tighter than four times its diameter installed (eight for shielded), about 1 in for Cat6, and is pulled at no more than 25 lbf; tighter bends, crushing staples and long untwisted ends at the jack degrade crosstalk performance, which a certification tester shows and a continuity tester does not. Cable is supported by the structure (800.24) and is not strapped to electrical raceways for support (800.133(C)). Conduit outdoors is a wet location, so cable in it to a camera or gate is listed for wet locations.

Communications cable is separated from power by distance, a barrier or the power wiring method

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D7

Communications cable keeps 2 in from open light and power conductors (800.133(B)), a rule that does not apply where the power conductors are in a raceway or in a Chapter 3 cable such as NM, AC or MC, which covers most house wiring. In a shared box or enclosure a barrier separates them. The noise a 120 V branch circuit induces in a twisted pair is small at house currents; where low-voltage cable crosses power wiring it crosses at a right angle, and long parallel runs in one bore are avoided as practice. Class 2 lighting conductors follow their own separation rule (Lighting · Low voltage).

Each crossing of a fire separation or the air barrier is sealed

CableEquipmentPathway

Informs D7

Penetrations of the floor between stories and of the top plates into the attic are fireblocked with an approved material (CRC R302.11 item 4), and penetrations of the separation between an attached garage and the house are protected the same way (R302.5.3); 800.26 adds requirements only where an assembly is fire-resistance-rated, which a one-family house's floors and garage separation usually are not. The same openings at the ceiling are leaks in the air barrier, and the sealant that fireblocks them also closes the air path (Insulation · Air barrier). A house heated and cooled by water with an ERV has no air-handler plenum, so plenum-rated cable is needed only in other spaces used for environmental air; cable is not run inside an ERV duct (300.22(B); HVAC · Duct topology). Back-to-back outlet boxes in a partition between rooms are a sound path; offsetting them by a stud bay avoids it (Soundproofing · Leaks).

Watch out

A sleeve left open at both ends is a chimney between floors and a leak into the attic; sealed around and inside with a removable fireblocking sealant, it stays both closed and reusable.

8 · Code, permits and documentation

Which code applies

The 2025 California Electrical Code, the 2023 NEC with California amendments, governs communications wiring from 1 January 2026. The 2023 edition reorganized Chapter 8: Article 800 now holds general requirements for communications systems, including cable listings, grounding and bonding, and fire spread; Article 805 holds communications circuits, Article 820 coax for television and cable services, and Article 770 optical fiber; the installation and separation rules for all of them now sit in 800.133. Power-limited cabling moved into Articles 722 (cables) and 725 (Class 2 and 3 circuits). No code requires structured cabling, an outlet count or a protocol in a dwelling; ANSI/TIA-568 and TIA-570 are industry standards. Figure 8 shows which rules a run engages; Table 9 lists them.

Permits and documentation

Many building departments treat low-voltage communications wiring as permit-exempt and inspect it only where it crosses rated assemblies or accompanies electrical work; the model residential code exempts equipment operating under 25 V and not capable of supplying more than 50 W; PoE runs at 44–57 V, so no PoE circuit falls within that text, and Types 3 and 4 also exceed 50 W. The receptacle circuit for the distribution point is electrical work under the permit. At rough-in an inspector sees firestopping at floor and plate penetrations, garage-separation penetrations, box and enclosure separation from power, and the bonding conductor; at final, the intersystem bonding termination and the protector. A record of each run, its label at both ends and a certification test result is the as-built document for the cable plant.

Where it goes wrong

  1. Outlets daisy-chained or coax split in a tree, so one fault darkens several rooms (Part 2).
  2. The distribution point placed at the service by default, lengthening every run and putting active equipment in a garage or an exterior closet (Part 2, Part 5).
  3. A provider protector bonded with a conductor longer than 20 ft and no separate electrode, which fails inspection (Part 2).
  4. An active PoE switch and recorder in a closed in-wall media can, which runs past its rating and shortens equipment life (Part 5).
  5. The distribution point's circuit left out of the backed-up loads, so cameras, alarm communication and the storage gateway drop in an outage (Part 5).
  6. One access point on the ground floor for the whole house, leaving the upper floor at 2.4 GHz or on a wireless hop (Part 6).
  7. No sleeve or pull string at floor and plate crossings, so the next cable means opening finished walls (Part 7).
  8. Unsealed penetrations at floors, the attic plate and the garage wall, which fail the fireblocking inspection and leak air (Part 7).
  9. Cables stapled tight, kinked or strapped to pipes and ducts, which pass a continuity test and fail certification at speed (Part 7).
  10. Old telephone and coax left abandoned in walls opened by the remodel, which the code requires removed where accessible or tagged (Part 8).

Glossary

Channel
The whole Ethernet path between two devices: permanent link plus patch cords, up to 100 m.
Distribution point
The enclosure or rack where home runs terminate on a patch panel; TIA-570 calls it the distribution device.
Demarcation point
Where the provider's network ends and the owner's wiring begins, usually at the protector or ONT.
Home run
One cable from an outlet directly to the distribution point, with no splice or tap.
ISBT
Intersystem bonding termination: the terminal bar at the service for communications bonding conductors (250.94).
LP cable
Cable listed for limited power and marked with a current per conductor, for PoE bundles.
MoCA
Ethernet carried over in-house coax by adapters.
ONT
Optical network terminal: the provider's device converting fiber to Ethernet.
Permanent link
The installed cable from patch panel to outlet jack, up to 90 m.
PSE, PD
Power sourcing equipment (the switch port) and powered device.
