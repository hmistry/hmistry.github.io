1 · Heat flow: what R-value describes and what it omits

Bottom line. Resistances add in series and conductances add in parallel by area, and the number on a product label covers conduction through one uniform layer only.

Resistances add in series; conductances add in parallel

R_path = Σ Rᵢ U_assembly = Σ fⱼ / R_pathⱼ Q = U · A · ΔT

R resistance, h·ft²·°F/Btu · fⱼ area fraction of path j · Q heat flow, Btu/h

layers along one path stack their resistances; side-by-side paths share the area and the weakest dominates.

R-value is measured in steady state on a uniform layer. Four things that matter in a house are outside it:

  • Framing in parallel with the insulation (Part 2).
  • Non-uniform thickness, such as the taper at a truss heel (Part 3).
  • Air moving through or around air-permeable insulation (Part 4).
  • Radiation across air spaces and heat storage, which shape summer attic gains (Part 3).

Film and layer values used throughout: interior films R-0.68 (vertical), 0.61 (heat flow up), 0.92 (heat flow down); exterior film R-0.17; softwood R-1.25 per inch; RSI = R ÷ 5.678.

The envelope on one axis: UA by component

Putting every surface in UA (Btu/h·°F) shows where heat leaves before any cost or effort enters. Air leakage enters as a UA equivalent, 1.08 × cfm. For the reference house (2,300 sf, two stories, R-13 walls, 300 sf of original clear dual-pane glazing, 6 ACH₅₀, garage cavities taken as empty) the total is about 873 Btu/h·°F (Figure 1).

2 · Walls: the framing caps a 2×4 assembly

Bottom line. Over a whole elevation about a quarter of a 2×4 wall is wood, and that path sets a floor on U that no cavity fill can pass.

Watch out

Closed-cell foam in a 2×4 cavity gains little over a batt because the framing still governs (Table 3), and it blocks inward drying, bonds both faces of the wall together and is not reversible.

3 · Ceiling and attic: depth, the heel and summer gain

Bottom line. A vented truss attic can take deep insulation cheaply in the field and very little at the eave, and above about R-38 depth buys little winter energy.

A standard truss heel thins the insulation at the eave

Ceiling and atticExterior wallsGarage boundarySlab

Informs D1

Where a truss bears on the wall, the space between the top plate and the roof sheathing is the heel height, about the top-chord depth (3½–5½ in) for a standard heel. CRC R806.3 keeps 1 in clear under the sheathing at the vents, so a 4 in heel leaves 3 in of insulation at the wall line. Depth then grows at the roof pitch (Figure 4).

d(x) = (h − c) + s·x U_band = ln[(a + bL)/a] / (L·b) a = R_nc + r·(h − c), b = r·s

h heel height · c vent clearance · s roof slope · r R per inch · L band width · R_nc films and gypsum

insulation depth grows linearly from the wall, so the band's average U is a logarithm rather than the field value.

Watch out

Under R806.3 every vented bay keeps a baffle and 1 in of clearance. Insulation blown against the soffit closes the vents that are the attic's moisture control, and baffles and depth dams attach to sheathing, plates or chord faces, never to a cut web (Framing · No truss member is cut).

4 · Air and the boundary: where the envelope is, and where it leaks

Bottom line. The insulation and the air barrier have to sit on the same continuous surface; where they separate, the insulation is bypassed.

The air barrier is a continuous plane, coincident with the insulation

Fibrous insulation is air-permeable, so it insulates only where a continuous air barrier sits against it. At the ceiling the barrier is the gypsum plane with its penetrations sealed; in walls it is the gypsum and the sheathing with their edges and penetrations sealed; at the garage it is the gypsum fire membrane. An insulation layer with an open air space on its warm side forms a ventilated cavity with little resistance.

Leakage is rated by a blower-door test as ACH₅₀ (air changes per hour at 50 Pa). Houses of this era typically test at 5–8 ACH₅₀. The flow at other pressures follows a power law.

Q = C · ΔPⁿ C = Q₅₀ / 50ⁿ n ≈ 0.65

Q airflow, cfm · ΔP pressure difference, Pa · n flow exponent (0.5 for a sharp orifice, 1 for laminar)

leakage grows less than in proportion to the pressure driving it.

At the ceiling, stack-driven leakage rivals conduction

Ceiling and atticExterior wallsGarage boundarySlab

Informs D1

ΔP = 0.0342 · P_atm · h · (1/T_out − 1/T_in) UA_leak = 1.08 · Q

h height above the neutral pressure plane, m · T absolute temperatures, K · P_atm 101,325 Pa

warm air is lighter than outdoor air, so the top of the house is pushed outward harder the colder it is outside.

The neutral plane sits where inflow and outflow balance. With 35% of the leakage in the ceiling plane, typical for the vintage, and the rest spread over the walls, it rises to about 3.8 m in a 5.5 m two-story house, and at 70 °F inside and 35 °F outside the ceiling sees about 1.4 Pa (Figure 7). A 6 ACH₅₀ reference house then pushes about 63 cfm through the ceiling, a UA equivalent of 68 Btu/h·°F against 48 for R-30 conduction and 34 at nominal R-49. Placing the neutral plane at mid-height, a common simplification, gives 87 cfm and is an upper bound.

Recessed downlights and partition top plates are usually the two largest categories of ceiling leakage (Figure 8). Fixture type and count are a lighting decision; the partition head under trusses is a joint designed to move (Framing · Truss uplift). Airtightness and stiffness are separate properties, so a flexible continuous seal closes that joint for air and sound at once (Soundproofing · The partition-head gap).

The boundary is one closed surface between conditioned and unconditioned volume (Figure 9). Interior partitions and the floor between stories are inside it and carry no thermal requirement. The garage is inside the footprint and outside the boundary, so the walls it shares with the house and the floor of the rooms above it are boundary surfaces, mandated today at R-15 for 2×4 walls and R-19 for floors. 5 · Moisture: drying direction and vapor control

Bottom line. In IECC zone 3 a stucco wall can dry toward the interior, and a low-perm interior layer removes that path; moisture reaches the attic by air, not by diffusion.

Two climate-zone systems share digits and mean different things

Title 24 energy climate zones (16 zones, by ZIP code in Reference Joint Appendix JA2) set energy values. The IECC/ASHRAE 169 zones set the CRC moisture rules: most of coastal and inland California is 3C (warm-marine) or 3B, with Marine 4 on parts of the north coast. CRC R702.7 requires an interior Class I or II vapor retarder only in Marine 4 and zones 5–8, so a Title 24 "zone 4" house is usually not a "zone 4" house for vapor purposes.

A vapor-open interior lets a stucco wall dry inward

Ceiling and atticExterior wallsGarage boundarySlab

Informs D2

Stucco stores rain and dew, and sun drives that moisture inward in warm weather. A vapor-open interior (unfaced fill, latex-painted gypsum, Class III at about 3–10 perms) lets it leave; a low-perm interior layer stops it at the back of the gypsum (Figure 10). In winter the outdoor side is not cold for long enough to drive significant outward condensation.

6 · Ground and radiant: slab edge, heated slabs, emitter backing

Bottom line. An unheated slab loses mostly at its edge and needs little insulation in a mild climate; a heated slab or an emitter panel needs insulation behind it to deliver its heat to the room.

An unheated slab loses heat at its edge

Ceiling and atticExterior wallsGarage boundarySlab

Informs D4

Q = F · P · ΔT

F slab-edge coefficient, Btu/h·ft·°F · P exposed perimeter, ft

slab loss is set by the length of edge exposed to outdoor air, not by the area.

Soil under a slab warms toward the slab temperature, so the field loses little and the edge dominates. F is about 0.73 for an uninsulated unheated slab and about 1.35 for an uninsulated heated one (ASHRAE 90.1 Appendix A, typical). Title 24 Table 150.1-A requires no slab-edge insulation in climate zones 3–4 for an unheated slab, which matches the mechanism. A slab continuous under an attached garage also loses sideways into the garage slab, a path the perimeter coefficient does not include.

A heated slab or panel loses a share of its output through its back

Informs D4

parasitic = q_back / (q_back + q_room) q_back = (T_emitter − T_back) / R_back

R_back resistance from emitter to the cold side

the share lost behind an emitter is set by the resistance behind it relative to the resistance to the room.

A slab delivering 15 Btu/h·sf to a 70 °F room runs near 80 °F at the tubing. Over ground at about 60 °F the field of an uninsulated slab loses about a quarter of its output, and the perimeter band, which loses toward outdoor air (35 °F at design), about two thirds; a new topping over R-10 board loses under a tenth (Figure 11). A ceiling panel follows the same rule against the attic: insulation in contact keeps the loss to 8% or less, the eave taper raises it to 20%, and an air gap open to the attic to about 63%. 7 · Interior cavities: fill for sound and fire

Bottom line. Most new fill in an interior remodel goes into partitions and the floor between stories, where it serves acoustics and fire, not heat.

Cavity fill for acoustic assemblies: airflow resistivity, not density

Fibrous fill damps the cavity resonance of a double-leaf wall and absorbs standing waves (Soundproofing · Lowering f₀). The governing property is airflow resistivity σ: below about 5,000 Pa·s/m² sound passes through, above about 30,000 the surface reflects, and the common products sit inside that band.

8 · Code, permits and documentation

Bottom line. Title 24 Part 6 binds insulation work, is inspected, and engages component by component as the work touches each one.

Which code, which cycle

The 2025 California Energy Code (Title 24 Part 6) applies to permit applications filed on or after 1 January 2026, with the CRC (Part 2.5) setting ventilation clearance, vapor retarders, fire separations and foam barriers. AB 130 (2025) bars changes to residential building standards until 1 June 2031, so this cycle is likely to govern through most remodels started now. Local reach codes adopted before the freeze may still apply.

§110.8(g) makes heated slab floors subject to Table 110.8-A on every compliance path, with R-5 in climate zones 1–15. The listed geometries are vertical insulation at the slab edge to 16 in or the frost line, or vertical plus 4 ft horizontal between slab and foundation; both presuppose excavation or a new pour. Insulation in contact with the ground must also limit water absorption (0.3%, ASTM C272, tested without facings) and vapor permeance (2.0 perm/in), which XPS and some high-density products meet and ordinary EPS does not; board laid over an existing slab under a topping is not in ground contact.

A topping over continuous board is thermally stronger than either listed geometry but is not one of them, so its acceptance is an equivalence question for the building department, settled at plan check rather than at inspection.

Where it goes wrong

  1. Insulating over an unsealed ceiling. Loose fill does not stop air; it buries the leaks and makes the attic surfaces above them colder, where exfiltrating air leaves its moisture (Part 4).
  2. Sealing the partition head rigidly, or not at all. Hard caulk cracks with the first winter's truss movement; an open joint is a leak and a sound path (Part 4).
  3. Blocking eave vents with new depth. Without baffles the fill closes the soffit vents and violates the 1 in clearance (Part 3).
  4. Reading nominal R as assembly performance. R-15 batts make an R-12 wall; R-49 at a standard heel is about R-40 (Part 2, Part 3).
  5. Batts compressed or gapped. A ¼ in gap at a stud or a batt split behind a box sets up convection; field studies place poor installations 20–30% below nominal, which is what QII inspects.
  6. A vapor retarder because of "zone 4". The Title 24 zone is not the IECC zone; kraft or polyethylene on the warm side of a zone 3 stucco wall removes its drying path (Part 5).
  7. Missing the garage boundary. Two surfaces inside the footprint carry about a quarter of the reference UA and are a CO path (Part 4).
  8. A gap behind a radiant panel. Insulation present but not in contact costs over half the output and passes inspection (Part 6).
  9. Spray foam in a decoupled partition. It bonds the leaves and gives back the decoupling (Part 7).
  10. Ordinary EPS as perimeter insulation in ground contact. It fails the §110.8(g) absorption limit (Part 8).
  11. Exposed kraft facings in an attic or garage fail the §110.8(c) flame-spread limits unless in contact with a finish; inspectors check this at the insulation inspection.
  12. Closing the ceiling before the fixture plan is final. Every later downlight is a new hole in a buried plane (Part 4).
